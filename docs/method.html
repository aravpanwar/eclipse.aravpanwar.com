Totality Path & Nearby Locations

Method
All eclipses

What this is

For each total solar eclipse on this site, every populated place inside the path, with how long totality lasts there and what that costs against the longest totality anywhere on the path. Places within 100 km outside the path are listed too, with how much of the sun is covered there.

Every eclipse is computed the same way. Where a figure below belongs to one eclipse, it is for 2 August 2027, the next one.

Eclipse geometry

Local circumstances are computed from the Besselian elements published by NASA GSFC, following the method in the Explanatory Supplement to the Astronomical Ephemeris (1974) and Meeus, Elements of Solar Eclipses (1989). Nothing is traced from a map.

Delta T

Each eclipse is computed with the ΔT on NASA’s Besselian elements page for it: 71.7 s for 2 August 2027. ΔT is not perfectly predictable and shifts the path east or west. NASA’s eclipse search engine gives 76.0 s for 2027, which moves the path by about 2 km, and versions of this site before November 2026 used that value. The figure used is stated in each dataset, so you can tell which one the numbers assume.

Lunar limb

The Moon has mountains, which move the path limits by 1 to 2 km and duration by 1 to 3 s against the smooth-limb figures used here. Limb-corrected predictions are usually published 12 to 18 months before an eclipse.

Greatest eclipse is not greatest duration

These are different points. Greatest eclipse is where the shadow axis passes closest to the centre of the Earth; greatest duration is where totality lasts longest. For 2027 they are 0.6 s and about 250 km apart. The site shows how long totality lasts at each place, not how much it falls short. That gap is in the CSV download as seconds_sacrificed, measured against greatest duration, which is the honest denominator.

Local times

Times are local clock time at each place: the computed moment of maximum eclipse, less half the duration for the start and plus half for the end, in the place’s time zone from GeoNames. They are worked out when the dataset is built, with the rules of the IANA time zone database, so they include summer time: Egypt is on UTC+3 in August 2027, not UTC+2. Morocco moved to permanent UTC+0 on 20 September 2026, so Moroccan times for 2027 here are an hour earlier than in sources made before then. Times are truncated to the minute, and the Moon’s mountains can move the start and end by a second or two.

Distances

All distances are great-circle. Circle membership is by distance, not by testing points against the drawn circle polygon, which would make places on the edge flicker in and out as you zoom. The radius is straight-line, and straight lines are not journeys: in 2027, 120 km along the Nile and 120 km into the Western Desert are very different drives.

Validation

The computation is checked against independently published values before anything downstream is trusted. For every eclipse, NASA’s greatest eclipse point must lie on the computed shadow axis, with the Sun’s altitude within 0.3° and the duration within 2 s of NASA’s figures. 2 August 2027 is checked in more detail:

Places

From GeoNames cities1000, filtered to populated places and administrative seats. Sections of a town are left out, since GeoNames lists them as places of their own and they would list one town twice. Every country is searched, and the eclipse calculation decides which places are in totality. Coverage is uneven by country: in 2027, 118 Spanish places inside the path against 18 Libyan and 8 Somali. That is a fact about who edits GeoNames, not about where towns are. A place missing here is missing from GeoNames.

Population figures come from GeoNames as published and are not always the town: for some Egyptian entries they are the surrounding district, which is why Esna appears larger than Luxor.

Towns outside the path

Every place in the same GeoNames list that is within 100 km of the edge of totality but not inside it: for 2027, 1,062 places in 13 countries. They see a partial eclipse only. They are on the map as hollow dots and join the list when you search or drop a pin. They have no cloud or ranking, and are in a CSV of their own.

For each one the site shows how much of the sun is covered at the peak, and the local time of the peak. The share covered is the part of the sun’s disc hidden by the Moon, by area. At 99% the sky dims, but the day stays bright and the corona does not appear: only totality shows it.

Two distances are given: to the nearest point on the edge of totality, and to the nearest point on the centre line, where totality is longest. Both are straight-line distances, measured to the path as drawn on the map, and are not road distances.

A partial eclipse is never safe to look at without eclipse glasses or a proper solar filter, even at 99%. Only totality itself is safe to look at with the naked eye.

Cloud

ERA5 total cloud cover, 1985 to 2024, sampled at each place’s local eclipse hour on the eclipse date and the day either side. That is 120 observations per place, not 120 independent years, since consecutive days in one month are correlated. Cloud is added for each eclipse closer to its date, so later eclipses show none yet, and eclipses that have passed carry none.

ERA5 runs on a 0.25 degree grid, about 28 km. That is coarser than the terrain: in 2027, one cell can span the Nile valley and the desert plateau beside it, which do not have the same weather. Read these numbers as regional.

2027 is checked against Jay Anderson’s satellite-derived figures at eclipsophile.com: Tarifa computes at 23.8% against his 26%, close agreement from an independent method and dataset. Luxor computes at 3.1% against his 0.7%, and Melilla at 25.3% against his 39%; both read high for the grid-resolution reason above. For a specific site Anderson is the better source. His tables are not reproduced here because they state no licence.

Climatology is not a forecast. A real forecast for an eclipse day exists about a week beforehand and is worth more than any of this.

Travel advisories

From the UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office, fetched when the dataset was built and shown with the date the FCDO last reviewed it. The advisory is attached to the country group, never to individual places, and nothing is hidden or reordered because of one. Where the FCDO names the specific regions an advisory covers, those are listed, because “parts of the country” without saying which parts is not useful. Eclipses that have passed carry no advisories, since today’s advice says nothing about a trip that can no longer be made.

On the map, a country under an advisory against all travel is shaded: red when the advisory covers the whole country, pink when it covers parts. The FCDO publishes those parts as names, not outlines, so the pink covers the whole country. Country outlines are from Natural Earth, in its UK point-of-view edition to match the source of the advisories.

What is not here

  • Places to stay. This is not a booking site. Each town links to a map search for places to stay around it instead. A count of what OpenStreetMap has mapped said little about what can be booked, and how complete the mapping is could not be checked well enough in most countries.
  • Prices. Deliberately. They have a shelf life of weeks.
  • Drive times. Road data in several of these countries is too thin to support an estimate worth showing.
  • An attention or popularity signal. Tested against Wikipedia pageviews and dropped: only 28% of these places have an English article with meaningful traffic, and the ratio it produced ranked obscure towns as more overpriced than famous ones.
  • Tracking. Visits are counted with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data. There are no accounts and no ads.
